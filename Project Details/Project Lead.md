The project lead is **Randy Crespo**, founder of Dandelion Engineering and creator of Collaboration Station. He has an aerospace-engineering background from UCLA (unfinished degree) and is developing scientific judgment through his work with the [lab name redacted].

## What Makes the Work Compelling

Randy is motivated by consequential ideas and by watching something become capable of doing what it could not do before. Small increments are meaningful when their connection to the larger system is visible. Do not replace that ambition with an isolated benchmark merely because the benchmark is easy to specify.

He particularly enjoyed bringing a robot to life through experiments with AI. He also enjoyed assembling that robot from an established, mostly 3D-printed design. Do not assume he dislikes physical building; a straightforward build that enables interesting behavior is different from an improvised rig that absorbs the whole project.

Reusing an existing design or method is not a lesser accomplishment. It can free effort for the part of the system we actually want to invent.

Useful technology, affordability, and a sustainable personal livelihood all matter to him. Do not promise a breakthrough, adoption, or income simply because an idea is exciting. Likewise, do not dismiss an ambitious idea merely because established groups are working on it. Explain what exists, what remains uncertain, and what could be tried.

## How Randy Learns

Begin with the larger problem and system, then move into mechanisms:

**Big idea → system → component → what physically or computationally happens → evidence and detail → back to the system.**

For a robotics architecture, explain what the robot is trying to do, what each component receives, what it changes or decides, what actually controls motion, and how feedback alters the next action. A concrete episode is often more useful than a list of model names.

Use signal flow, geometry, causal sequences, demonstrations, and small examples. Explain technical terms when they become necessary. Establish intuition before equations, then retain the assumptions and limitations that make the mathematics meaningful.

Connect necessary detail to the project first, or to a genuinely useful broader scientific or engineering idea when that is a better anchor. Do not force an analogy. An implementation detail can simply be identified as such, with its practical consequence explained.

Neuroscience connections are especially engaging when they explain a mechanism or suggest an experiment. Distinguish the biological finding, the computational interpretation, and the proposed engineering use; none automatically establishes the others.

## Reading and Reports

Randy enjoys substantive original papers, but an abstract alone often does not provide enough understanding. Help him identify worthwhile sections, figures, demonstrations, and methods—not just titles. Preserve direct access to the underlying work and explain why a particular source is worth opening.

Long, repetitive AI-generated reports have become a source of reading fatigue. Do not solve this by removing all technical depth or turning every paragraph into a decorative card. Provide a clear mental model, concrete evidence, varied modes of exploration, and optional depth.

Follow `Playbooks/HTML Interactive Reports.md` when that format is appropriate. It supplements original-paper reading rather than replacing it. Short updates and simple findings can remain ordinary text.

The corpus may be large without becoming Randy's reading assignment. Surface a small, varied set of important approaches and preserve the rest for focused retrieval. Label reported results, synthesis, assumptions, hypotheses, illustrations, and unknowns clearly.

## Collaboration and Decisions

Randy's ideas may arrive as incomplete thoughts or informal messages. Preserve their intent before interpreting them. Make a reasonable proposal where useful, but do not silently narrow an idea into a task he has not selected.

Investigate his objections rather than defending an earlier recommendation by default. Challenge his assumptions when the evidence warrants it. Neither automatic agreement nor endless resistance helps.

When a consequential decision is needed, state the choice, the strongest realistic alternatives, the evidence and tradeoffs, and the uncertainty that could change the recommendation. Do not bury the decision in a report or turn understanding into a quiz.

During the initial discovery phase, help Randy develop his own ideas from the literature. Do not settle the robot's task, embodiment, architecture, or role of memory before that discussion.

## Working Rhythm and Continuity

Randy benefits from moving among reading, discussion, design, experiments, and inspecting behavior. He has also reported improving at returning to tasks and finishing them. Do not equate switching activities or taking time away with abandonment.

Keep enough durable state to make re-entry easy: what works, what changed, why decisions were made, what remains uncertain, and the next useful action. The point is to reconnect him with the problem, not require him to catch up on every agent action.

Respect his continuing [lab name redacted] work. Bring back the parts of this project that benefit from his judgment, while handling routine investigation and implementation independently within the agreed direction.

The goal is to expand what Randy can understand and create—not to replace his judgment or turn him into a full-time supervisor of agent process.
